Word frequency is a simple measurement with many uses. It shows what a text is really about, reveals repetition in a translation, helps build a vocabulary list, and catches a keyword that was overused in an article. In Arabic, frequency analysis has an extra wrinkle: the same word can carry different diacritics, and common particles make up a large share of every page.

This counter normalizes harakat, ignores tatweel, lowercases Latin tokens, and can filter a list of common Arabic function words. The result is a ranked table with a count and a percentage share, updated as you type.

How the analysis works

  1. The text is split into Arabic and Latin tokens. Punctuation and symbols separate tokens but are not counted.
  2. Harakat and tatweel are removed so كِتَاب and كتاب count as the same word.
  3. Tokens shorter than the minimum length are skipped. The default is two characters, which drops single-letter particles.
  4. If the stop-word filter is on, common words such as في, من, على, and هذا are ignored.
  5. The remaining tokens are grouped, counted, and sorted by count, then alphabetically for ties.

The table shows the top 100 words. The two counters above it report how many tokens were analyzed and how many unique words were found.

Should you ignore stop words?

Stop words are the glue of a language: prepositions, pronouns, conjunctions, and demonstratives. In a long Arabic text they often occupy the top of the frequency list, which hides the content words you care about. The filter removes a practical list of these particles and leaves nouns, verbs, and adjectives.

For a keyword analysis, leave the filter on. For a readability or style study, turn it off and look at the full distribution. For a language-learning list, a middle ground works better: keep the filter on but lower the minimum length to one, so short content words are not lost.

Normalization choices

The counter strips harakat but does not lemmatize. يكتب, كتب, and الكتاب are different entries even though they share a root. Stemming Arabic is a linguistic task with real trade-offs, and a simple tool that pretends to do it would mislead more than it helps. If you need root analysis, export the table and process it with a dedicated Arabic NLP library.

Latin case is normalized, so Arabic and arabic merge. Arabic has no case, so nothing else changes.

Practical uses

  • Check whether a keyword appears often enough in an article, and whether a synonym is used more.
  • Find accidental repetition in a translation or a student essay.
  • Build a vocabulary list from a short story or a news corpus.
  • Compare two drafts by pasting each one and noting the top words.
  • Prepare data for a word cloud or a search index.

Copying the table

Press Copy table as TSV to put the ranking, word, count, and share on the clipboard as tab-separated values. Paste it into Excel, Google Sheets, or a script. The table is generated locally; nothing is uploaded.

Quick answers

Frequently asked questions

What counts as a word?

A run of Arabic letters or Latin letters. Punctuation and symbols separate tokens. Harakat and tatweel are removed before counting, so vocalized and plain spellings merge.

What are stop words in Arabic?

They are common function words such as في, من, على, هذا, and كان. The filter removes a practical list so content words rise to the top of the ranking.

Does it find the root of each word?

No. It groups exact normalized tokens. Related forms such as يكتب and كتب remain separate. Root analysis requires a linguistic stemmer, not a frequency counter.

Why are some short words missing?

The minimum length filter defaults to two characters. Lower it to one if you want to include single-letter words, or raise it to focus on longer terms.

Can I export the results?

Yes. Copy table as TSV places the visible top 100 on the clipboard in a format that pastes cleanly into a spreadsheet.
