Remove Arabic diacritics

Arabic harakat remover

Turn fully vocalized Arabic into plain text by removing the vowel marks, without touching a single letter.

The tool removes fatha, kasra, damma, tanwin, sukun, shadda, and the superscript alef. Letters and punctuation stay untouched.

Everything runs locally in your browser. Nothing is uploaded to a server.

Harakat are the small marks above and below Arabic letters that indicate short vowels, doubling, and the absence of a vowel. They are essential for children, poetry, the Quran, and any text where pronunciation is ambiguous. They are usually omitted in newspapers, websites, and everyday writing.

This tool removes every harakat mark in one pass. It is useful when a vocalized source must match an unvocalized house style, when a search index needs plain letters, or when diacritics copied from a religious text interfere with word counting.

What is removed

  • Fatha َ, kasra ِ, and damma ُ.
  • The three tanwin forms: ً, ٍ, and ٌ.
  • Sukun ْ, which marks a consonant with no vowel.
  • Shadda ّ, which marks a doubled consonant.
  • The superscript alef ٰ, used in a few words and in Quranic orthography.

Letters, spaces, punctuation, digits, and tatweel are not affected. The output is the same sentence with the marks lifted off.

When to remove harakat

Most modern Arabic writing is unvocalized. If you are publishing a news article, a product page, or a social post, removing the marks gives the text the expected look and avoids inconsistent vocalization. If you are preparing text for search, stemming, or frequency analysis, removing harakat lets كِتَاب and كتاب count as the same word.

Do not remove them from a Quranic passage, a poem, a grammar lesson, or a children's book without a specific reason. The marks carry meaning and are part of the intended reading experience.

Mark order and shadda

In properly encoded Arabic, a shadda comes before the vowel it accompanies: مُحَمَّد has the shadda followed by a fatha on the same letter. Some copied text has the order reversed. This remover simply deletes both, so the letter remains and the word stays valid. If you need to keep vocalization, use the keyboard's diacritic cleanup instead, which normalizes the order.

Harakat and word counts

Word counters that count characters often treat a fatha as a character, which inflates the total. Removing harakat first gives a count that matches the visible letters. The word and character counter can measure both versions if you want to compare.

Before and after

Vocalized

ذَهَبَ الطَّالِبُ إِلَى الْمَدْرَسَةِ مُبَكِّرًا.

Unvocalized

ذهب الطالب إلى المدرسة مبكرا.

Readability changes; the words do not. For a search index or a plain-text export, that is exactly what you want.

Nothing leaves the page

The removal runs as a regular expression in your browser. There is no server, no account, and no history. Copy the result before closing the tab if you need it later.

Quick answers

Frequently asked questions

Does the remover change the letters?

No. It deletes only diacritic marks. Every Arabic letter, space, number, and punctuation mark stays exactly where it was.

Is shadda removed too?

Yes. Shadda is a diacritic, so it is removed along with the short vowels. If you need to keep doubling information, do not run the tool on that text.

Can I remove harakat from only part of the text?

The tool processes the whole editor. Copy the section you want to clean into the editor, run it, then paste the result back into your document.

Why would I remove harakat from a search index?

Removing the marks makes vocalized and unvocalized spellings match, so a search for كتاب also finds كِتَاب. It also reduces the character count and avoids duplicate tokens.

Does it keep the superscript alef?

No. The superscript alef is treated as a haraka and removed. The base alef remains if it is present in the text.
