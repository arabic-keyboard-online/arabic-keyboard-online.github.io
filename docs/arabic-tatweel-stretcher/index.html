Justify Arabic the old way

Arabic tatweel and kashida tool

Stretch Arabic words with valid kashida joins, or strip every tatweel character from text that already has too many.

Stretched text

Kashida is added only after letters that can connect to the next letter, so the word stays typographically valid.

Everything runs locally in your browser. Nothing is uploaded to a server.

Tatweel, also called kashida, is the horizontal line that extends a connection between two Arabic letters. Typographers use it to justify a line, balance a heading, or give a logo a deliberate rhythm. In Unicode it is the character U+0640, and it is a real character rather than a font feature, which means it survives copy and paste but can also accumulate in text where it was never intended.

This tool does two jobs: it inserts kashida only where a letter can legally connect to the next one, or it removes every tatweel character from a block of text.

What kashida can and cannot do

Arabic letters connect on a baseline. Some letters connect only to the preceding letter, not to the following one: ا, د, ذ, ر, ز, و, and a few others. You cannot stretch after those letters without breaking the word. The tool checks the next letter and inserts kashida only after a connecting letter, so بيت can be stretched between ب and ي, but دار cannot be stretched between د and ا.

The result is typographically valid but not a replacement for a proper justification engine. A layout program distributes kashida with aesthetic rules; this tool applies a mechanical, even insertion.

Adding kashida

Choose one, three, or five marks per join. One mark is subtle and works for body text. Three or five marks create a visible stretch for a title or a poster. The converter removes any existing tatweel before adding new marks, so repeated clicks do not multiply the length.

Harakat are preserved and stay attached to their letter. The insertion happens after any marks, so a fatha is not separated from its consonant by the stretch.

Removing kashida

Switch to Remove and the output drops every U+0640 character. This is useful for text copied from a justified PDF, where the kashida used for alignment becomes visible garbage in a new layout, or for search fields where the extra character prevents a match. Removing tatweel does not change the letters or the meaning.

When to stretch and when not

  • Headlines and logotypes: a controlled stretch can make a short Arabic title fill its line.
  • Print justification: for a paragraph, prefer the justification engine in InDesign or a CSS text-align setting. Manual kashida in body text quickly looks uneven.
  • Formal documents: avoid it. Modern Arabic typesetting rarely uses manual kashida in contracts or reports.
  • Cleanup: remove it when a PDF export leaves visible gaps or when a search fails on an otherwise identical word.

Copying the result

The output updates live. Press Copy and paste into your design or document. Tatweel is a normal Unicode character, so it displays in any Arabic font, though the visual length depends on the font's design. Check the result in the final font before approving a print file.

Accessibility note: screen readers may read tatweel as a pause or ignore it. Do not use it to fake spacing in a sentence that will be read aloud.

Quick answers

Frequently asked questions

What is tatweel or kashida?

It is the horizontal line that stretches the connection between two Arabic letters. In Unicode it is U+0640. Typographers use it to justify lines and balance short headings.

Why are some letters not stretched?

Letters such as ا, د, ذ, ر, ز, and و do not connect to the following letter. Inserting kashida after them would break the word, so the tool skips those positions.

Can I remove existing tatweel?

Yes. Choose Remove kashida. Every U+0640 character is deleted while letters, harakat, and punctuation remain.

Does the tool preserve harakat?

Yes. Marks stay attached to their letter, and the kashida is inserted after them so the vowel is not separated from the consonant.

Is manual kashida good for body text?

Usually not. Use the justification engine of your layout program for paragraphs. Manual kashida is best for short headings, logos, and deliberate display typography.
