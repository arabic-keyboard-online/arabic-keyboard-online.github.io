Punctuation that fits the script

Arabic punctuation converter

Swap Latin commas, question marks, and semicolons for their Arabic equivalents, or go back the other way, without touching numbers.

Arabic punctuation

Everything runs locally in your browser. Nothing is uploaded to a server.

Arabic has its own punctuation marks. The comma is ،, the question mark is ؟, and the semicolon is ؛. They occupy the same role as the Latin marks but use different Unicode characters. A document that uses , and ? inside Arabic text is readable, yet it looks foreign to Arabic typesetting and may fail a simple character check.

This converter changes only those three marks. It leaves letters, spaces, numbers, quotes, and brackets alone. You can go from Latin to Arabic or from Arabic back to Latin, and the result updates as you type.

Why use Arabic punctuation?

Punctuation is part of the script's visual grammar. The Arabic comma sits on the baseline and points in the direction of the text; the Latin comma hangs below it. In a right-to-left paragraph, the Arabic question mark mirrors the Latin shape. Using the correct marks produces a page that looks native to Arabic readers, whether it is a school essay, a product label, or a subtitle.

There is also a technical reason. Search engines, screen readers, and text-to-speech engines can use punctuation to decide where a sentence ends. A Latin question mark inside Arabic may be spoken with a different intonation or ignored by a language model trained on Arabic text.

Exclamation marks, colons, periods, parentheses, and quotation marks are not changed, because Arabic commonly uses the same characters for those. That keeps the conversion focused and avoids surprising side effects.

Decimals stay intact

The comma rule uses a negative lookbehind and lookahead: a comma is converted only when it is not between two digits. A French decimal comma in 3,14 or a thousands separator in 1,250.00 remains a Latin comma. This is important when the text contains prices, measurements, or coordinates.

If your Arabic text legitimately uses a Latin comma between digits as sentence punctuation, the tool will skip it. That is rare, and the alternative, corrupting every number, is worse.

Converting back to Latin

The reverse direction is useful when an Arabic sentence must be pasted into an English document or a form that only accepts ASCII punctuation. It converts ، to a comma, ؟ to a question mark, and ؛ to a semicolon. The text direction is a separate matter; this tool changes characters, not the paragraph direction.

The output box stays right-to-left so Arabic remains readable, but you can copy the content into a left-to-right field. If the field displays the sentence in the wrong order, that is a bidirectional layout issue in the destination app, not a punctuation problem.

A safe, local conversion

Type or paste on the left, read the result on the right, then press Copy. The conversion runs in JavaScript in your browser. Nothing is uploaded, and the editor is cleared when you reload the page.

Quick answers

Frequently asked questions

Which punctuation marks are converted?

Only the comma, question mark, and semicolon, in both directions. Periods, exclamation marks, colons, quotes, and brackets are left as they are.

Will my decimal commas be changed?

No. A comma between two digits is not converted, so 3,14 and 1,250.00 remain unchanged. Only sentence commas become the Arabic comma.

Can I convert Arabic punctuation back to Latin?

Yes. Choose the Arabic to Latin direction. The tool replaces ، with a comma, ؟ with a question mark, and ؛ with a semicolon.

Does it change the direction of the text?

No. It only changes characters. The output box remains right-to-left for readability; direction in another app depends on that app.

Is the conversion instant?

Yes. The result updates as you type or paste, so you can check each change before copying.
